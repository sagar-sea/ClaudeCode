Everyone's resume says "prompt engineering."

Almost no one can answer the one question that's quietly deciding who gets hired:

"Walk me through an eval you designed."

If you can't answer that off the top of your head, you're not alone — and that's exactly why it works. Hiring managers have started using it at every level, because it separates people who've actually shipped an AI system to production from people who watched a lot of YouTube.

You can fake a prompt. You can fake a demo. You cannot fake the story of how you measured whether your agent actually worked, what it got wrong, and how you knew.

That's eval design. And it's become the highest-signal skill in AI hiring.

Why now? Because the applied-AI companies everyone wants to work at — Perplexity, Cursor, Harvey, Sierra, Cognition — hire evals engineers among their first ten technical people. Their reasoning is blunt: eval velocity determines product velocity. If you can't measure quality fast, you can't ship fast.

Here's the good news: it's learnable, and a portfolio proves it better than any certificate. Concretely:

→ Pick one agent you've built (even a toy one).
→ Design an eval dataset that hunts for its REAL failure modes — not the easy cases it already passes.
→ Learn one framework: Inspect, Promptfoo, Braintrust, or LangSmith.
→ Add the judgment layer: know when "LLM-as-judge" is trustworthy and when it quietly lies. This is the rarest, most valuable skill of all.

Then write up what you found. That single case study — "here's how I measured my agent, here's what broke, here's what I fixed" — is worth more than ten lines of tools on your resume.

Anyone can build. Few can prove it works. Be the one who can.

What's the last thing you shipped where you actually measured whether it worked?
